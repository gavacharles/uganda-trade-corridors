A 40-tonne truck leaves Kampala for the Kenyan border at Malaba. On an open road it would burn 86 litres of diesel. On the real A1 it burns 147.

The difference is the road's second life. Uganda's trade corridors were built as highways, but along most of their length they are now village high streets: trading centres, boda boda stages, markets, schools, junctions every few hundred metres.

I mapped every 500 m of the five main roads out of Kampala, about 1,700 km, using only open data. What I found:

🛣️ 56–74% of each corridor now runs through roadside settlement. Open road is down to 18–36%, and buildings within 300 m of the road grew another 22–48% between 2016 and 2023.

⏱️ A loaded truck loses 59–161 minutes per trip to that friction. The five worst stretches are all weighbridges, at 10–12 minutes each.

⛽ Stop-and-go driving burns about US$70 M of extra diesel a year across the five roads: roughly 138,000 tonnes of CO₂.

🚸 65–79% of the road-safety risk sits in the villages between towns, where trucks still run at 55–60 km/h. That is also where police place their crash black spots.

🧭 The bigger delay is off the road. Tracked trucks take 17–93 hours from Kampala to a border; road friction is only 2–14% of that. Borders, rest stops and checks take the rest.

The cheapest wins are at controls: weigh-in-motion screening (8–16 minutes saved per truck trip) and ending routine police-post stops (4–15). Service roads through the busiest settlement belts cut delay, fuel and risk together. And the stretches that are still open need building control now, before they become the next trading centre.
