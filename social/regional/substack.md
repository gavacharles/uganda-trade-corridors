# Substack: Leaving the capital

**Title:** Leaving the capital
**Subtitle:** I followed 54 main roads out of 12 African capitals. East Africa's trade roads have become towns, and a truck pays for it in time, diesel and lives.
**Cover:** media/cover_regional.png (1200 × 630)
**Interactive map:** https://gavacharles.github.io/uganda-trade-corridors/regional.html

---

Put a loaded truck on the road from Nairobi to the Ugandan border. Put an identical truck on the road from Pretoria to Zimbabwe. Same distance, 440 km. Same tarmac. Both end at a busy border.

The Kenyan truck arrives more than three hours behind schedule on roadside friction alone. The South African truck loses less than an hour and a half.

[media: rx1_two_corridors.mp4]

Nothing dramatic causes the difference: no crash, no border queue, no pothole. It is death by a thousand slow-downs. A market spills onto the shoulder. A minibus stops to load. A motorcycle taxi pulls out of a side road. A speed hump guards a school. Then come a police check and a hill. The Kenyan truck passes about 73,000 buildings within 100 metres of the road. The South African truck passes about 8,900.

Earlier this year I measured this on Uganda's five trade roads out of Kampala ([link to the Uganda piece]). It left me with an obvious question: is Uganda unusual? So I ran the same measurement on every main road out of twelve hubs in ten countries.

## 54 roads, 15,000 km, every 500 metres

The hubs are five in East Africa: Kampala, Nairobi, Kigali, Dodoma and Dar es Salaam. The other seven are in Southern Africa: Pretoria, Johannesburg, Gaborone, Harare, Lusaka, Maputo and Windhoek. I didn't pick the roads by hand. A script follows every numbered national route that leaves each city to its end or to the border. That gives 54 roads and about 15,000 km. For a fair comparison I use the first 200 km of every road.

Every road is cut into 500-metre pieces, about 30,700 of them. For each piece, from open data, I measured:

- the buildings beside it;
- the side roads joining it;
- the markets, signals, crossings, speed humps, police posts and weighbridges along it;
- the hills;
- twenty years of daily rain;
- the people living within 300 metres.

A travel-time model then converts all of it into minutes for a loaded truck, run a thousand times across the range of every assumption. A fuel model converts it into diesel.

You can explore all of it yourself, every 2 km stretch, down to street level on satellite imagery: **[open the interactive map](https://gavacharles.github.io/uganda-trade-corridors/regional.html)**.

## Twice the delay

[media: rx3_country_ranking.mp4]

On the median East African road, a loaded truck loses 35 minutes per 100 km to the roadside. On the median Southern African road it loses 18. Rwanda (47), Mozambique (42), Kenya (38) and Uganda (35) are slowest. Namibia (15) and Zimbabwe (12) are fastest.

The worst trade corridors are the two roads that meet at Malaba, on the Kenya–Uganda border: Kampala–Malaba at 58 minutes per 100 km and Nairobi–Malaba at 45. Together they form the spine of the Northern Corridor, the busiest transit route in East Africa. The quickest are Harare–Beitbridge (6) and Windhoek–Walvis Bay (7).

Here is how 100 km of road builds up, cause by cause, with every road in each region pooled:

[media: rx8_cause_waterfall.mp4]

Friction adds about a third to a truck's open-road driving time in East Africa, and about a fifth in Southern Africa.

## The roads have become towns

[media: rx9_road_type_reveal.mp4]

This is the heart of it. About two-thirds of the length of East Africa's main roads now runs through roadside settlement or town: continuous roofs, markets, taxi stages, and a side road every few hundred metres. In Southern Africa it is about a third, and outside the cities most roads still cross open country.

East African roads have a median of 155 buildings within 100 m per km of road. Southern African roads have 34. Across all 54 roads, roadside building alone tracks lost time closely (a correlation of 0.73). Where a Southern African road does run through dense settlement, such as Maputo to the border or Lusaka to Chirundu, it loses time like an East African one. This is about settlement, not about regions.

And the roadside is still filling in:

[media: rx2_roadside_growth.mp4]

Built-up land within 300 m of East Africa's roads grew by about half between 2000 and 2020. In Southern Africa it grew by about a third. Most of it is in the villages between towns, not in the towns. Up close, at the fastest-growing stretch in each region, it looks like this:

## Diesel and carbon

Every slow-down has to be paid back with diesel, because a 40-tonne truck burns fuel to get back up to speed.

[media: rx6_fuel_meter.mp4]

Over those same 440 km, the Kenyan truck burns about 114 extra litres to friction and emits about 300 kg of extra CO₂. The South African truck burns 28 litres. Across the region, friction costs a loaded truck a median of about 11 extra litres per 100 km in East Africa and 9 in Southern Africa: a fifth to a third of its fuel. This is the part of transport emissions that no engine standard will fix.

## People beside the road

The roadside is where people live. About 1,200 people live within 300 m of every km of East African main road, against 270 in Southern Africa.

The risk is not mainly in the towns, where speed limits and crowds slow trucks down. It is in the villages between them, where trucks still run at close to open-road speed past homes, schools and markets.

[media: rx7_safety_sweep.mp4]

On the Nairobi–Malaba road, a truck passes about 160,000 people at more than 50 km/h, and 31 school stretches with no marked crossing. On Pretoria–Beitbridge the figures are about 39,000 people and 2 school stretches. Per km, East African roads carry about 2.5 times the road-safety exposure.

## One honest caveat

OpenStreetMap, the open map I use for signals, crossings and checkpoints, is mapped in great detail in South Africa and patchily in East Africa. Ugandan and Tanzanian towns have traffic lights and zebra crossings too; far fewer of them are on the map.

[media: rx10_mapping_gap.mp4]

So part of Southern Africa's apparent "signals problem" is a mapping story. That is why the headline comparison leans on buildings, side roads and hills, which come from satellite data the same way everywhere.

## What would fix it?

[media: rx12_fixes.mp4]

Most corridor investment goes to borders, ports and pavements. Those matter. A truck still spends far longer waiting at a border or in a rest stop than it loses on the road. But the moving part of the trip is not free, and in East Africa it is getting slower as the roadside fills in.

Three things would help:

1. **Fix the control points.** Weigh-in-motion at weighbridges and an end to routine police stops recover as much time per trip as long lengths of new road, at a fraction of the cost.
2. **Treat the worst stretches.** Service roads, bays for buses and taxis, and protected crossings near schools take local life off the through lane without pushing trucks faster through villages.
3. **Plan the frontage.** New roads and bypasses attract the same settlement within a decade unless the land along them is planned.

Together, the fixes I modelled recover about a fifth of truck delay in both regions. The rest is the price of roads that have become towns. Southern Africa's problem is different: its slow roads are its metropolitan exits, where freight routing and signal priority would do more.

## The fine print

- The travel-time model was built and checked in Uganda, and its assumptions are applied everywhere. Trust the comparisons more than the absolute minutes.
- Congestion isn't modelled; these are light-traffic numbers.
- Speed humps are assumed in towns, because almost none are mapped.
- One truck count is used for every road, so I don't compare yearly totals.

The full research paper, every map and all the code and data are open: https://github.com/gavacharles/uganda-trade-corridors.
